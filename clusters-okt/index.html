Virtual factories and AI factory digital twins in 2026

So what is a virtual factory? A virtual factory is a digital twin of an entire plant: a 3D model of the building, machines, robots, conveyors, workers and logistics flows that is fed with real data and used to plan, test and run production before anything changes on the shop floor. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) describes digital twin technology as the creation of “electronic representations of real-world entities” that let you view their states and how they change over time. Applied to a factory, this means planners can move a production line, add a robot or change a shift pattern in software and see the effect first.

BMW shows what this looks like at scale. In June 2025, the carmaker reported that its planners were working in digital twins of more than 30 production sites, that a virtual collision check ahead of a new model launch now takes three days instead of almost four weeks of real testing, and that its Virtual Factory is expected to cut production planning costs by up to 30 per cent. In January 2026, Siemens said that PepsiCo, working with its new Digital Twin Composer, achieved a 20 per cent increase in throughput on initial deployment and could identify up to 90 per cent of potential issues before making any physical changes.

And what is an AI factory digital twin? The term is used in two ways. The first is a factory digital twin with AI built in, in which AI agents and robots are trained and tested in the simulation before they work in the real plant. In October 2025, NVIDIA expanded its “Mega” Omniverse Blueprint with libraries for factory-scale digital twins, with Siemens as the first software developer to support it and companies such as Foxconn, Toyota, TSMC, Lucid Motors and Caterpillar building factory twins with Omniverse. The second meaning is a digital twin of an ‘AI factory’, NVIDIA’s term for a data centre built to produce AI. Its Omniverse Blueprint for AI factory digital twins lets engineering teams design, simulate and optimise an entire data centre, including power, cooling and networking, in a physically accurate virtual environment before it is built.

For manufacturers, the lesson is that the digital twin is moving from a planning tool to the place where production is designed, tested and increasingly steered by AI. AI in schools in 2026

AI in schools in 2026 is no longer a question of whether, but of how. The OECD’s TALIS 2024 survey of teachers in 55 education systems was the first large international study of how teachers use AI. When the results came out in October 2025, Singapore’s Ministry of Education reported that on average 36 per cent of teachers across the OECD used AI to teach or support student learning, compared with 75 per cent in Singapore. On average, 53 per cent agreed that AI helps teachers write or improve lesson plans, and 45 per cent that it helps them automate administrative tasks.

Rules are catching up. In the European Union, the AI Act has banned emotion recognition in education institutions since 2 February 2025, and it classifies AI that may determine someone’s access to education or the course of their professional life, such as the scoring of exams, as high-risk. Strict obligations for such high-risk systems apply from 2 December 2027. In the United States, an executive order signed on 23 April 2025 made it federal policy to promote AI literacy “by promoting the appropriate integration of AI into education”, set up a White House Task Force on Artificial Intelligence Education and told the Department of Education to prioritise AI in its teacher training grants.

What should pupils actually learn? In June 2026, the European Commission and the OECD published an AI Literacy Framework for primary and secondary education. It sets out 19 competences, organised around knowledge, skills and attitudes, across four dimensions: engaging with AI, creating with AI, managing AI and shaping AI. The framework also complements the PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy assessment, which will measure how well students around the world understand and use AI.

Taken together, the six examples above show what is possible, and these frameworks show what is expected: teachers who are trained to use AI well, clear rules on which tools pupils may use and for what, and lessons that explain how AI works rather than only how to use it.
