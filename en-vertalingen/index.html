Rituals case study: target audience and The Birthday Wall

Richard van Hooijdonk · Engelse versie · concept

The Birthday Wall, Rituals' 2011 Facebook campaign, targeted brand fans aged 25 to 45 who like to take care of themselves, plus their friends. It reached over a million people and produced almost 3,000 Birthday Walls. The brand has grown a lot since: in 2025 Rituals reported net revenue of EUR 2.43 billion, up 16% year on year (Rituals).

This is the English version of a Dutch case study first published in 2011. Figures about the campaign are from 2011; the campaign website is no longer online.

From 16 June to 15 August 2011, Rituals ran a cross-media social campaign called The Birthday Wall. It is an interesting case in which social media played a leading role. We spoke to the people behind the campaign and turned what we learned into this case study.

The campaign had one main goal:

  • Rituals wanted to inspire Facebook fans around the world to turn birthday wishes back into a meaningful ritual.

Secondary goals:

  • Increase online visibility
  • Grow the online fan database

This case study covers:

  • Campaign goals
  • Introduction and motivation
  • Target audience (social persona)
  • Media selection
  • How it worked
  • Results
  • Our feedback

Introduction and motivation

The Rituals philosophy is about turning everyday routines into meaningful rituals. For 2011, the brand wanted to put one important social ritual in the spotlight. It chose the birthday, because it suspected that social media had turned birthday wishes into more of a routine than a ritual. A survey among Rituals fans confirmed this. That was the reason for Rituals to create The Birthday Wall and give the birthday ritual back its meaning.

Target audience (social persona)

Primary target audience

  • Consumers who love the Rituals brand
  • Consumers who take good care of themselves
  • Aged between 25 and 45
  • Members of Facebook

Secondary target audience

  • Friends of the primary target audience

The campaign focused mainly on the Netherlands, but it also ran in these countries:

  • Belgium
  • England
  • Germany
  • Spain

Media selection

In April 2011, Rituals surveyed its online fans. The main aim was to map how fans celebrate birthdays, a form of marketing intelligence.

Among other things, the survey showed that 56% of respondents congratulate their friends via social media. Of these, 29% said that doing so via social media is "nice and easy".

How did it work?

  • Surprise your friends on Facebook with a special Birthday Wall on www.bdaywall.com
  • Leave a birthday message. This could be a note, a photo and/or a kiss. You could even choose the colour of your own tile
  • You could also eat a slice of digital cake... and share it with your friends on Facebook
  • Invite the birthday person's friends to leave a message too
  • With ten or more messages, the birthday person received a Rituals gift worth EUR 9.90 by post

The campaign was promoted through, among other things, in-store materials, video, the website, newsletters, social media, press releases, bloggers, interviews, partners and Facebook Ads.

Results

After the campaign ended in 2011, Rituals reported these results:

  • More than 30,000 visitors to the campaign page
  • Almost 3,000 Birthday Walls created
  • 11,000 birthday messages left
  • 1,075,000 people reached by the campaign

Our feedback on this cross-media social campaign

  • In line with human-centred marketing, the campaign used the power of social media: giving friends genuine attention and so strengthening the relationship (the social aspect).
  • The focus was on encouraging people to do the same for their own friends (the viral aspect).
  • Hyves, then the largest Dutch social network, was used to promote the campaign, but the campaign itself could not be used on Hyves. In 2011, Hyves had a potential reach of roughly 3.5 million people in the Netherlands within the selected age group (25 to 45), about 0.5 million more than Facebook. (Hyves closed as a social network on 2 December 2013; on 22 September 2026 Mediahuis launched a new Hyves.)
  • The campaign used a simple five-step plan, which was clearly explained on the campaign site.
  • To earn the gift, at least ten friends had to actually take part. These had to be ten mutual friends, which in our view was too many and hard to reach.
  • There was no mobile version (app) of the campaign site, even though many people in the target audience used the mobile internet. This slowed the viral effect, as many smartphone owners would have seen the campaign on their phones. The birthday walls themselves could be viewed on a smartphone.
  • There was no blog to share the campaign's progress or to put birthday people in the spotlight.

Google's 5 most important acquisitions

Richard van Hooijdonk · Engelse versie · concept

Google's five most important acquisitions are arguably DoubleClick, YouTube, Android, Boston Dynamics and Waze. The advertising and video deals still pay off: in 2025, YouTube's revenue from ads and subscriptions exceeded 60 billion dollars, according to parent company Alphabet. Boston Dynamics is now majority-owned by Hyundai.

In the 15 years up to 2014, when this article was first published, Google had spent 28 billion dollars buying 163 companies. They covered every corner of technology, from robots to facial recognition and from gaming to security. Of those 163 companies, five stand out to me as the deals that made Google what it is today: one of the most powerful companies in the world.

DoubleClick: online advertising, 3.1 billion dollars

Google agreed to buy DoubleClick in April 2007 for 3.1 billion dollars in cash and completed the deal in March 2008. Online advertising has been Google's main source of income ever since. Figures for the second quarter of 2014 show revenue of almost 16 billion dollars that quarter, of which 14.4 billion dollars (90%) came from advertising. Google is now part of parent company Alphabet, and advertising is still by far the biggest source of income: in the fourth quarter of 2025, 82.3 billion of Alphabet's 113.8 billion dollars in revenue came from Google advertising, according to Alphabet's quarterly results.

YouTube: video, 1.65 billion dollars

In October 2006, Google bought the world's largest video platform for 1.65 billion dollars in shares. By 2014, YouTube had around 1 billion unique visitors a month, making it the most used search engine in the world after Google itself. In 2025, YouTube brought in more than 60 billion dollars from advertising and subscriptions, according to Alphabet.

Android: mobile software, 50 million dollars

Andy Rubin, then the owner of Android, sold his company to Google for around 50 million dollars. Less than ten years later, by 2014, Android had become the most widely used operating system for mobile phones in the world.

Boston Dynamics: robotics, 500 million dollars

Before Google bought the company, the Pentagon was one of its most important customers. When this article appeared in 2014, I expected that many people would benefit from robots taking over simple tasks, and that taxi drivers, rubbish collectors and bakers would disappear within 10 to 15 years. Google later sold Boston Dynamics again. Since June 2021, Hyundai Motor Group has owned 80% of the company, after an acquisition from SoftBank that valued it at 1.1 billion dollars. For more on where robots stand now, read why we need robots in every industry and why the future may be more about working alongside cobots.

Waze: navigation software, 966 million dollars

In June 2013, Google completed its acquisition of the GPS and navigation company Waze, for a total of 966 million dollars in cash. Waze had not yet made a profit at the time, but for Google the value lay in the crowdsourced data of its 44 million users.

A microchip in my hand

Richard van Hooijdonk · Engelse versie · concept

A microchip in your hand is a small RFID or NFC implant that can open doors, unlock a phone or store data, much like the chip in a contactless bank card. Richard van Hooijdonk had one implanted by 2015. Employers cannot simply demand one: since June 2026, Washington State has banned employers from microchipping employees.

This article is based on an interview in the Dutch newspaper De Telegraaf from 2015. The quotes are translated from Dutch.

Doors open by themselves when Richard van Hooijdonk comes within 30 centimetres. The same goes for his Tesla. In 2015, he expected that the electric car would soon even start automatically when the NFC chip was nearby. It is all possible thanks to the radio chip the Amsterdam-based trendwatcher had implanted in his hand.

In 2015, a few hundred people around the world were walking around with a chip like this. Van Hooijdonk can also use it to open a safe and unlock his phone. Besides keys, the chip stores passwords and bitcoins. He expects that in the future he will be able to pay contactless by waving his hand over a card reader. Technically, that is no great feat, because a bank card contains the same kind of radio chip (RFID). "You can connect pretty much anything to it, and it is more accurate than biometrics or fingerprints."

As a frequent speaker on striking trends, he wants to experience first-hand how this technology works. He also has the software it needs programmed himself. Van Hooijdonk also sees chips and sensors coming that continuously monitor his health. There is room for them in the teeth and elsewhere in the body.

Besides the chip, the iPhone 6 was his most important companion in 2015. The Braintoss app sends his many ideas to his to-do list. TextExpander is handy too. "Communicating on the go becomes much easier with shortcuts. You can save bits of text under a code. When you type the code, the whole text appears."

Marketing intelligence: the beating heart of your business

Richard van Hooijdonk · Engelse versie · concept

Marketing intelligence is the systematic collection and analysis of knowledge about your customers, campaigns, website, communities and market, so you can make better decisions in time. Start with the data you already have, add what you need and choose suitable tools. Mind the rules: the EU Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025.

This is the English version of a Dutch article first published in 2012. Tools mentioned as examples are dated.

The beating heart of every organisation is what it knows about its customers, the campaigns it runs, the website it maintains, the communities it manages and the market it operates in. That knowledge helps us make the right choices and adjust course in time. So start by mapping the data you already have. Then decide what extra data you need to manage well. Finally, make sure you use the right tools to collect and report this information.

Today's technology lets us capture customer data intelligently, combine it with other data (from social media, for example) and measure (online) campaigns in detail.

1) What do we want to know about our customers?

We want to know our customers better than just their name, address, phone number and email address. We want to know more about their purchase and visit history, customer value, income level, household, interests, internet and other media use, presence on social networks, click behaviour and so on. In short: all data that could be relevant. Bear in mind that the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has applied since 25 May 2018 and sets rules for collecting and combining personal data. List all of this and look for "hooks" for good analysis, for example to predict customer behaviour or potential customer groups.

In 2012, social CRM functionality already made it possible to link social media data to customer data. That way you know what your customers are up to and you can prepare (sales) conversations well. You can respond to their situation and needs in a relevant way.

Examples of social CRM software in 2012 were Sprout Social, Optify and further developed existing systems such as Salesforce and SugarCRM.

2) Measuring campaigns

You cannot tell whether a campaign is successful if you cannot measure it. After all, you want to know whether the campaign was worth the investment compared with what it brought in. The return determines whether you want to repeat a similar campaign.

  • Decide what you want to measure per campaign: conversion, sales, enquiries, customer spending, awareness, social mentions, sentiment and so on.
  • Decide how you can measure the result: set up clear reporting or a presentation (a "dashboard").
  • Analyse: are the results in line with expectations? Adjust where needed.

3) Analysing your website

The website is increasingly the foundation of the (commercial) strategy of companies, institutions and organisations. The better you steer how customers and prospects use the website, the more meaningful it becomes, both socially and commercially. Non-profit organisations, just like commercial ones, want to achieve their goals. They need people to choose them. For non-profits, the gain might be new donors, fundraising, awareness or behaviour change.

Because the website matters so much, we want to measure how it performs.

  • Decide what you want to measure on your website: online conversion, sales, enquiries, reviews, ratings, newsletter sign-ups, number of visitors, time on site, drop-offs and so on.
  • Use web analytics: get insight into the data above and other relevant data with web analytics. Examples from 2012: Google Analytics, Sitestat and Omniture. The version of Google Analytics used at the time (Universal Analytics) has stopped processing data since 1 July 2023; its successor is Google Analytics 4.
  • Analyse: are the results in line with expectations? Adjust where needed.

4) Analysing communities

Communities on (social media) channels send traffic to your website, where the conversion and so on take place. That is why it pays to do the same exercise for your communities on Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn and other platforms as you do for your website. In 2012, Crowdbooster.com was an interesting free tool for analysing your Twitter account. It showed, among other things, how many people saw and retweeted your tweets and how your follower numbers grew.

5) What do we want to know about our market, partners and competitors?

Consumers change and new trends emerge. Society develops, and so do competitors, suppliers, customers and other business partners. Services are launched, campaigns are tested, decisions are made and mistakes are absorbed. By looking and "listening" around you, you stay sharp and find inspiration.

  • Which themes or developments do you want to follow? Which competitors, business partners and other market players do you want to monitor?
  • Use "listening tools": Google Alerts, Hootsuite, Radian6 and Meltwater are examples of tools for following market developments based on keywords. Use RSS readers to follow news on relevant (industry) sites. Create regular reports from the information you collect. Relevant information can be general news about industries and competitors, but also questions on Twitter from other users ("Who is the best supplier for service X?").
  • Prioritise and make choices: decide which developments are of great (commercial) importance and act on them.
  • Carry out your strategy: create projects, pilots, assignments, cases, polls, questions and answers. Use the information you gather to support your chosen strategy or to adjust the existing one.
  • Analyse: check regularly whether the information you collected has led to successful execution of your strategy and the related projects. Adjust your information needs where necessary.

Conclusion: measuring = growing!

Marketing intelligence adds a lot of value. It shows you how best to find, connect with and win back customers. Marketing intelligence is the heart that keeps your organisation beating. More insight leads to healthy growth. So I hereby change the well-known Dutch saying "meten is weten" (to measure is to know) into: measuring = growing!

Meta-omschrijving (147 tekens)In 2014 robots like Pepper, Jibo and Watson made headlines. Which jobs would robots take over, which new ones would appear, and what did that mean?

Help! The robots are running off with my job!

Richard van Hooijdonk · Engelse versie · concept

Robots will take over some jobs and create others, such as robot designer and data scientist. The pace is high: about five million industrial robots were working in factories worldwide in 2025, according to the International Federation of Robotics. When this article first appeared in 2014, there were around 1.3 million.

This is the English version of a Dutch article first published in October 2014. Predictions and figures are from that time and are marked as such.

In 2014, the Dutch Minister of Social Affairs, Lodewijk Asscher, brought big news: robots are going to take over our workplaces. A wonderful signal, and finally a way to make the general public aware of a development that will change societies and economies dramatically.

As early as 1964, Isaac Asimov predicted that robots would come. In 1980, Honda started the ASIMO project, which produced a fantastic robot. Things sped up enormously when Google turned its attention to robots. More than three years before this article, Google was already interested in Boston Dynamics, the maker of the fighting robot Petman and the support robot BigDog. Further development of artificial intelligence will lead to major breakthroughs in the coming years.

I think it is a wonderful development. Why should we stand at an assembly line or carry out complicated analyses when a machine or robot can do it faster and better? Robots are never ill, never ask for a pay rise and never struggle with motivation. We get more free time, and the robots earn a basic income for everyone. Could the future look like this?

What are robots?

Put simply, robots are machines shaped into something we recognise as human. The human-looking version is called an android: it looks like a person, but it is a robot. Robots are not new, by the way. We know the spambot, the factory robot and the iRobot vacuum cleaner. The last one cleans my house every day. Robots are also found in factories as industrial robots. In this article we mainly look at robots that serve people, and take them to a higher level.

Robot 3.0 is a smart computer with a friendly appearance, facial expressions and the ability to sense and empathise. It has sensors that produce data. This data is analysed, combined with existing data sources (recognition signals, for example) and turned into actions based on rules that can be set. A simple example: the robot recognises a sad face and senses a cold body temperature. It then says comforting words and puts an arm around you. Or a surgeon who is supported by a diagnostic robot that takes over the work of the diagnostic doctor. This robot can scan tissue and produce analyses faster, which it then presents to the surgeon.

Striking robots of 2014

Robot developers and their robots are practising hard. Steel alone is of no use to us. We need robots that can contribute directly to solving problems and challenges. These were some of the striking and valuable robots in 2014:

Fighting robot Petman

Built to go further than humans can in certain areas. For example, Petman can carry out research in a nuclear zone. Petman can make the same movements as a human. (VIDEO)

The Electronic Tongue

This robot can measure the quality of food. You give it a bite of food and ten sensors set to work measuring its chemical make-up. Using a score between 1 and 100, the robot shows whether the food passes the test.

Zora the care robot

In the care home where Zora works, residents adore it. In 2014 it cost 20,000 euros, but according to the management of the Boarebreker care home in Ostend (Belgium), it was absolutely worth it. Zora sings, talks, demonstrates exercises and does sums with residents. (VIDEO)

Family robot Jibo

Jibo is part of the family. It reminds Mum while she is cooking that she needs to pick up one of the children in half an hour, and it reads the children a bedtime story.

SaviOne the room service robot

Staff on the ground floor need to bring a toothbrush to room 465 on the 13th floor. They give SaviOne the toothbrush, enter the room number, and the room service robot finds its own way through the hotel. Once it arrives at the room by lift, the robot calls the room and delivers the toothbrush.

Companion robot Pepper

Pepper can look things up for you online, remind you of appointments and play with you. It recognises your emotions and will, for example, play a cheerful song when you are feeling down. It can also make small talk. All Peppers are connected through the cloud, so they learn from each other and keep getting better.

Watson, IBM's supercomputer

Back in 2011, this IBM supercomputer beat two clever contestants at the popular quiz show Jeopardy!. By 2014, it could not only process large amounts of data into, for example, a diagnosis, but also answer statements "rationally".

ASIMO (Honda)

This robot is 130 cm tall and weighs 54 kilos. ASIMO stands for Advanced Step in Innovative Mobility. By 2014 it had reached its fifth generation, and what was new in this generation was that its hands had five fingers. All of these fingers have sensors that can measure weight and force. (VIDEO)

Will robots take over your job?

Why should we stand at an assembly line? Why do work that robots can do better and faster? Because we enjoy it, or perhaps because we are used to it? Many occupations could disappear within a few years. Think of bus, taxi or lorry drivers. In 2014, I expected self-driving cars to be driving around the Netherlands in large numbers from the following year. The question then was whether we would still need human drivers ten years later.

What about telemarketers, actuaries, data entry clerks, accountants and tax advisers, or car salespeople? The list of jobs that robots could eventually take over is long. How fast robots are introduced will depend on technical progress and on how far society accepts robot 3.0.

In time, products will no longer be assembled by human hands either. In 2014, Foxconn, the maker of the iPhone, iPad and PlayStation, was working with Google on the Foxbot project. The Foxbot was meant to replace the work of 500,000 employees. A clear example of the old and new economy working together: Foxconn needed Google to speed up, and Google needed test environments to develop its robot technology.

Looking at industrial robots (see the previous example) that take over entire production processes, we could conclude in 2014 that this would not move very fast in the Netherlands. At that time, 1.3 million robots were working worldwide, compared with 1 million in 2008. Despite the crisis, the number of working robots had grown by 30% in just over five years. Japan had 300,000 robots, China 133,000, and the rest were spread across the US, Germany and Korea. The Netherlands barely took part, with only 7,400 robots, just half a percent of the world total. Whether robots in Japan and South Korea would really destroy jobs was open to question. Unemployment there was low and the working week long. In the short term, robots would not do much damage there.

What is clear is that robots will cost jobs. Certain occupations, such as drivers, lab technicians, bookkeepers, insurance agents, supervisors and pizza delivery riders, could disappear within 5 to 10 years, I wrote in 2014. Low-skilled jobs are under pressure, and these workers will need to look for opportunities elsewhere. At the same time, robot technology needs new, highly qualified people, such as robot designers and data scientists. These specialists need to be trained quickly, together with innovative businesses. We cannot start soon enough. One initiative is code weeks, in which primary schools spend several hours a week teaching young children modern computer science. History will repeat itself. The industrial revolution, and later the chip, led to job losses but also to new jobs.

Will robots be smarter than people by 2020?

In 2014, I expected that this could happen around 2020. By then, artificial intelligence would have to be advanced enough for robots to make their own decisions based on observations (sensors), data and algorithms. Robots and machines would be able to smell, feel and taste and, using algorithms, make choices on their own (even better than you and I together). In 2014, there was no need to worry about this in the short term: robots then had the IQ of a dog.

Google's acquisition of DeepMind will contribute to this. Neuroscientist Demis Hassabis sold his company to Google for 400 million. The company helps Google with remembering, predicting, discovering, participating, perceiving and categorising based on human perception. Google's goal, as I saw it in 2014, was to develop search engines with human knowledge that can upload memory to computers or robots.

Can we make ourselves smarter by upgrading our brains?

An interesting question that has been on my mind for some time. Last week I upgraded my iMac, while my brain (or our brains) has not had an upgrade in the last 50,000 years. Neuroscientists are working on this.

After studying a neurological experiment, I was convinced. Brain signals from a dog were transferred to those of a human. The dog was startled, and the signal was then transferred digitally to a test subject, whose startled reaction I could see on the video. If we can transfer our brain signals through connectors (APIs: application programming interfaces), it should be possible to consult the collective knowledge of many people and networks at the same time. We would then be on our way to a so-called real-life internet, where we can benefit instantly from linked sources (brains, databases, the web).

But we still have a long way to go to develop technology that can detect, transfer, analyse and activate 86 billion brain cells. A nightmare for many people, but fascinating and full of opportunity.

What are the consequences of the robot economy?

In general, using machines (and so robots too) delivers higher returns and lower risk. They offer continuity, low costs, long working hours and no (emotional) resistance. Start a conversation about this with random people and you get very different reactions: from fear to feelings of freedom and opportunity. Below is my 2014 prediction for people, businesses, societies, politics and government.

For people

For many people, a period of uncertainty is beginning. This uncertainty will grow in the coming years as robots play a clearer role in our lives. History shows, however, that people are flexible enough to adapt to even extremely changing circumstances. If robots take over a large part of the work, life will become cheaper. Products and services can be produced at lower cost. We work less and have more free time. Successful professions will be data scientist and robot programmer, until we develop robots that programme themselves. Robots will become commonplace: they will load the shopping, wash the car and pick the children up from school.

For businesses

Businesses will find a sound economic case for using robots. Production costs can fall by more than 75%, so companies that equip themselves with robots and smart machines can compete better with global players. Governments around the world will tax part of these profits heavily. Many companies will then move to countries where no such tax exists yet. Service companies will need more time to make robots suitable for personal service. Robots in public roles will eventually become commonplace, but still face a steep social learning curve. Only wealthy companies will benefit from robots, as robot technology will remain fairly expensive for the next ten years. Less wealthy companies will be pushed aside by the head start of others. Companies will be driven by high technology and will struggle to find scientists and programmers for the next 20 years.

For societies

Social structures and power relations will be tested. What place will the robot have in our society: human or machine? Societies will accept robots and eventually regard them as full persons. Android-like robots are almost impossible to tell apart from humans. They look, talk and smell the same. In time, the line between human and robot will disappear. Interesting food for thought for sociologists and anthropologists.

For governments and politics

Governments will better understand the effects of robots and data on the economy and society. Independent institutes will be set up together with businesses to help develop policy and regulation. These institutes will also be connected at a political level worldwide.

Governments will make the transition to the innovation economy, and ministers for innovation will be appointed. Education and innovation will get top priority in order to survive in fast-changing times. A good example is the code weeks for primary schools. In 2014, I described these as an initiative of Dutch European Commissioner Neelie Kroes; according to the organisers, EU Code Week was launched in 2013 by the Young Advisors for the Digital Agenda Europe. Where a driving licence or social skills are important requirements today, digital knowledge and affinity will probably rank higher on the wish lists of future employers. As for data security, we can expect every citizen to get a digital passport and a digital guardian that watches over personal data and personal identity and steps in when cybercrime threatens. Let's hope it won't be Google providing this guardian. This is a task for governments. A personal data vault will become mandatory: every citizen has the right to decide where their own data goes.

Conclusion

We can speculate freely about the effects robots will have on our lives. We may find it strange or frightening, but we must look at the future with fresh eyes. Robots can make life more pleasant and efficient. Society, politics and business set the conditions. Fear and anger, in this case about robots, are almost synonymous with innovation. Around 1790, we went from making products by hand to making them by machine, which led to major protests. The second industrial revolution has begun, with smart factories, robots and intelligent machines that take over intelligent tasks from people. In fact not much different from 1790, only smarter and better. And again there is disbelief and resistance.

Thirty years before this article, people in the Netherlands shouted "stop the chip". A million jobs would disappear. Many jobs, such as typists and word processor operators, did disappear. But the chip also brought many new products, large numbers of new jobs and economic growth in new sectors. The farmer milking his cow by hand is a thing of the past. The milking robot has given this part of agriculture a new future, and the Netherlands leads the world.

People, businesses and societies need decades to accept and implement major developments. We do not have that time. The biggest bottlenecks are data, security and public acceptance. Think about a distinctive product proposition

Richard van Hooijdonk · Engelse versie · concept

A distinctive product proposition helps you stand out even when you cannot change the core product. You build it in four layers: the core product, add-ons, online services and extras. IKEA shows how: in its 2025 financial year, Ingka Group, the largest IKEA retailer, counted 736 million store visits.

This is the English version of a Dutch article first published in 2009.

We cannot always change or improve the core product straight away. Yet we still need a distinctive product proposition to get ahead of the competition.

At my seminars in 2009, I often showed the striking example of IKEA, which sells its Billy bookcase in huge numbers. IKEA supports this basic product with a community (the IKEA club), a tasty restaurant, the ball pit, home delivery, a carefully planned stock policy and an assembly service. Customers are happy and feel they are getting a good-value, complete experience. Even though they have often spent a considerable amount on a family lunch, extra furniture and its assembly, IKEA food and the delivery service. That is the secret behind the hundreds of thousands of people who happily pay IKEA a visit every weekend, while IKEA comfortably watches the customer value of its target group rise.

After this IKEA detour, it is your turn to start developing a distinctive product proposition. Four elements play a role. First, the core product has to be right. We can extend this product with modules or add-ons that make the product or service "richer" and more distinctive, and so increase customer value. The third element is the option of adding online services. These support the basic proposition and help it stand out.

Finally, we define extras, such as gifts and other incentives that get the customer to press the "yes" button.
