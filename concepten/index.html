Een keynotespreker over AI kiezen: 8 vragen om vooraf te stellen

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

Een goede spreker over AI kies je niet op naam, maar op wat je zaal na afloop anders moet doen. Kennis is vaak het knelpunt: volgens Eurostat noemde 70,9% van de Europese bedrijven die AI overwogen maar niet gebruikten gebrek aan kennis. Stel vooraf acht vragen, onder meer over doel, publiek, voorbeelden uit je sector en prijs.

Dit artikel is een checklist, geen ranglijst. Je vindt hier geen "top 10 AI-sprekers", wel de vragen die je aan elke spreker of elk bureau kunt stellen.

Waarom boeken zoveel organisaties nu een sessie over AI?

AI is in korte tijd gewoon gereedschap geworden. Volgens Eurostat gebruikte in 2025 bijna 20% van de Europese bedrijven met tien of meer medewerkers AI, tegen 13,5% een jaar eerder. In Nederland was dat 33,2% (Eurostat meldt voor Nederland een trendbreuk in de cijferreeks van 2025).

Tegelijk lopen veel bedrijven vast op kennis. Van de Europese bedrijven die AI hadden overwogen maar niet gebruikten, noemde 70,9% gebrek aan kennis als reden, nog vóór onduidelijkheid over de juridische gevolgen.

Daar komt een wettelijke reden bij. Artikel 4 van de AI-verordening vraagt organisaties die AI inzetten om de AI-geletterdheid van hun mensen te ondersteunen. Die plicht geldt sinds 2 februari 2025, en sinds 2 augustus 2026 houden nationale toezichthouders er toezicht op, schrijft de Europese Commissie.

Welke 8 vragen stel je voordat je een AI-spreker boekt?

1. Wat moet de zaal na afloop anders doen?

Begin bij het doel, niet bij de spreker. Wil je dat mensen AI durven uitproberen? Dat het managementteam een besluit neemt over AI-agents? Of dat klanten je organisatie als vooruitstrevend zien? Elk doel vraagt een ander verhaal. Schrijf het in één zin op en leg die zin aan elke kandidaat voor.

2. Wie zit er in de zaal, en hoeveel weten ze al?

Een zaal met directeuren vraagt iets anders dan een personeelsdag met 800 mensen uit alle lagen. Ook het kennisniveau verschilt. In het Work Trend Index-onderzoek 2026 van Microsoft, met Nederland als een van de tien onderzochte landen, zegt maar 26% van de AI-gebruikers dat hun leiding duidelijk en consequent op één lijn zit over AI. Een goede spreker vraagt daarom eerst naar je publiek, voordat hij of zij een verhaal belooft.

Let ook op wat er niet hardop gezegd wordt. Veel medewerkers gebruiken AI al, maar zeggen het liever niet. Over dat verschijnsel schreven we eerder in AI-schaamte: hoe doorbreken we het?

3. Zoek je inspiratie, uitleg of allebei?

Een inspirerende keynote opent ogen en geeft energie. Een inhoudelijke sessie legt uit hoe iets werkt en wat je morgen kunt doen. De meeste organisatoren willen een mix, maar de verhouding verschilt. Vraag de spreker hoe die verhouding er bij jouw doel uitziet, en vraag om een fragment dat dat laat zien.

4. Hoe actueel is het verhaal?

Bij AI veroudert een verhaal snel. Volgens het AI Index Report 2026 van Stanford HAI steeg het slagingspercentage van AI-agents op de OSWorld-test, met echte taken op een computer, van 12% naar ongeveer 66%. Agents mislukken nog steeds bij ongeveer één op de drie pogingen. Een spreker die vorig jaar nog over chatbots sprak, vertelt nu een ander verhaal, of hoort dat te doen.

Vraag daarom: wanneer is de lezing voor het laatst bijgewerkt, en welke voorbeelden komen uit de laatste zes maanden?

5. Komen er voorbeelden uit jouw sector?

AI in de zorg is iets anders dan AI in de logistiek of in het onderwijs. Eurostat laat zien dat het gebruik sterk verschilt per sector: bedrijven in informatie en communicatie lopen voorop. Een zaal herkent zich pas als de voorbeelden uit de eigen wereld komen. Vraag welke sectorvoorbeelden de spreker gebruikt, en of die worden aangepast na een voorgesprek.

6. Is er ruimte voor interactie en vragen?

Vragen uit de zaal zijn vaak het nuttigste deel van een AI-sessie, omdat mensen dan hun eigen twijfels kwijt kunnen. Spreek af hoeveel tijd daarvoor is, of er een moderator is en of vragen ook vooraf of via een app kunnen. Bij een grote zaal werkt dat laatste vaak beter.

7. Nederlands of Engels, live of online?

Heb je internationale collega's of gasten, dan is een Engelstalige sessie soms beter. Vraag of de spreker in beide talen even sterk is. Bepaal ook de vorm: op locatie, online of hybride, en hoe lang. Kijk als voorbeeld hoe een lezingpagina over de AI-revolutie duur, taal en vorm beschrijft. Zulke gegevens horen op elke lezingpagina te staan, zodat je kandidaten makkelijk kunt vergelijken.

8. Wat kost een spreker over AI, en waar hangt dat van af?

Een vaste prijs is er niet. De prijs hangt meestal af van de duur, de locatie en reistijd, online of live, de hoeveelheid maatwerk en de bekendheid van de spreker. Vraag altijd wat er in de prijs zit: een voorgesprek, aanpassing aan je sector, een vraaggesprek na afloop of een opname. Vergelijk daarna niet alleen de prijs, maar ook wat je ervoor krijgt.

Telt een keynote mee voor de AI-geletterdheid van je mensen?

Een keynote kan helpen, maar is op zichzelf zelden genoeg. De Europese Commissie schrijft dat er geen vaste vorm is voor AI-geletterdheid: geen verplichte training en geen verplicht certificaat. Organisaties stemmen hun aanpak af op de kennis van hun mensen, de context en de risico's van de AI-systemen die ze gebruiken. Wel mag je bijhouden welke trainingen en initiatieven je hebt gedaan.

Een lezing is dan vooral een begin: ze maakt mensen nieuwsgierig en geeft een gedeelde taal. Daarna volgen workshops, afspraken over gebruik en oefenen in het eigen werk. Vraag een spreker gerust hoe de sessie in zo'n traject past.

Hoe controleer je of een spreker past?

  • Bekijk een recent fragment, liefst van de laatste maanden, niet een showreel van jaren terug.
  • Vraag een referentie van een organisator met een vergelijkbare zaal of sector. Reviews op de site van een spreker helpen bij een eerste selectie, zoals de reviewpagina van Richard van Hooijdonk, maar een eigen gesprek met een organisator zegt meer.
  • Plan een kort voorgesprek. Stelt de spreker vragen over je publiek en doel, of begint hij of zij meteen over het eigen verhaal?
  • Lees wat de spreker schrijft. Artikelen en nieuwsbrieven laten zien hoe actueel en zorgvuldig iemand werkt.

Wat zet je in de briefing?

Een goede briefing maakt elke spreker beter. Zet erin: het doel in één zin, wie er in de zaal zit, wat er vóór en na de sessie op het programma staat, welke vragen er nu leven, welke voorbeelden gevoelig liggen en wat je na afloop wilt meten. Wil je de zaal verder laten kijken dan AI alleen, geef dat dan ook mee. Denk aan de bredere ontwikkelingen uit vijf megatrends die het bedrijfsleven de komende tien jaar gaan opschudden.

Kort samengevat

Kies een spreker over AI op basis van je doel en je zaal. Vraag hoe actueel het verhaal is, of de voorbeelden uit jouw sector komen en hoeveel ruimte er is voor vragen. Vergelijk prijzen op wat erin zit. En zie een keynote als begin van het gesprek over AI in je organisatie, niet als het eindpunt.

What makes a good leader when AI agents join the team?

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

A good leader in a team with AI agents sets clear intent, decides what to hand to agents and what to keep human, makes it safe to experiment and owns the outcome. That matters more every month: according to Stanford HAI, agents went from 12% to around 66% success on real computer tasks.

Most lists of leadership qualities were written for teams of people. That is changing fast. This article looks at what stays the same, what matters more, and what a leader can start doing this quarter.

What changes when AI agents join a team?

An AI agent does more than answer a question. It takes a goal, plans steps and carries them out: it searches, fills in forms, writes code or handles a customer request. Agents are getting better quickly. According to Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index Report, agents went from 12% to around 66% task success on OSWorld, a test of real computer tasks. They still fail roughly one in three attempts.

That combination is the leadership challenge in one sentence: agents are good enough to rely on and not good enough to trust blindly.

Organisations are moving anyway. In Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index, 81% of leaders said they expect agents to be moderately or extensively integrated into their company's AI strategy in the next 12 to 18 months. Microsoft introduced the term "agent boss" for it: someone who builds, delegates to and manages agents.

Which leadership qualities matter more now?

Setting clear intent

When people do the work, a vague brief gets corrected along the way. An agent does exactly what it understood. The 2026 Work Trend Index, a survey of 20,000 AI users in ten countries, describes the new human role as setting clear intent: defining the desired outcome and the quality bar, and designing how the work is shared between humans and AI.

In practice: a good leader can say in two sentences what "done" looks like, and what "good enough" is not.

Judgement about what to delegate

If an agent fails one in three times on structured tasks, the question is not "can it do this?" but "what happens when it gets it wrong?". A leader decides where agents can work on their own, where a person checks the result, and where the work stays fully human. That choice depends on the cost of a mistake, not on how impressive the demo was.

Making it safe to experiment

This may be the most underrated quality. In the 2026 Work Trend Index, 45% of AI users said it feels safer to focus on current goals than to redesign work with AI. Only 13% said they are rewarded for reinventing their work with AI, even if the results are not met. Microsoft calls this the Transformation Paradox: people are ready, but the system around them still rewards the old way of working.

Managers make the difference. In a study of 1,800 workers cited in the same report, employees whose managers actively modelled AI use reported a 30-point lift in trust in agentic AI. Leaders who use the tools themselves, and say openly what did not work, give their team permission to try.

Alignment at the top

Only 26% of AI users in the 2026 survey say their leadership is clearly and consistently aligned on AI. When the top team sends mixed signals, every manager below makes different choices about agents, data and risk. A good leader makes sure the team knows the rules: which tools, which data, which decisions.

The growing role of agents also changes who holds knowledge in an organisation. We wrote about that in the democratisation of expertise.

Owning the outcome

An agent cannot be held accountable. People can. A leader who puts agents to work stays responsible for what they produce, for customers, colleagues and regulators. In Europe that responsibility is partly written into law. Under Article 4 of the EU AI Act, organisations that use AI must take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff. The European Commission explains that this obligation has applied since 2 February 2025, and that national authorities supervise and enforce it from 2 August 2026.

The classic characteristics of a good leader, and how they change

Ask people what makes a good leader and the same attributes come back: a clear vision, integrity, empathy, good communication, the courage to decide and the willingness to keep learning. None of these disappear when AI agents join the team. What changes is where they show up.

  • Vision becomes intent that an agent can act on: specific goals, boundaries and what "good" looks like.
  • Integrity now includes being open about what was done by people and what by software, and taking responsibility for both.
  • Empathy matters more, not less: people worry about their role when agents arrive, and a leader who listens keeps them on board.
  • Communication extends to explaining decisions that were prepared by an agent, in plain language.
  • Decisiveness means choosing what to automate and what to keep human, instead of leaving that to the tool.
  • Learning is no longer optional: leaders who use AI themselves understand its limits and earn more trust from their teams.

The best leadership traits are the same ones they always were. The difference is that they now have to work in a team where part of the work is done by software.

How do you lead a mixed team of people and agents?

A few practical habits help:

  • Name an owner for every agent. Someone who knows what it does, checks its work and can switch it off.
  • Agree on a human-agent ratio. Microsoft's 2025 report suggests asking two questions: how many agents are needed for which tasks, and how many people are needed to guide them?
  • Review mistakes openly. Treat an agent error like any other incident: what happened, why, and what changes.
  • Measure outcomes, not activity. An agent can produce a lot. The question is whether the work got better.
  • Invest in people first. In the 2025 Work Trend Index, upskilling the existing workforce (47%) was the top priority for leaders, just ahead of expanding capacity with digital labour (45%).

For the wider picture, read the six biggest AI leadership trends for 2026.

What stays the same?

Much more than the hype suggests. People still want a leader who is honest, listens, explains why, and treats them fairly. The traits in our overview of what makes a good leader have not expired. They now have a second audience: the way you design work for agents shows your team what you value.

AI can even help leaders do the human part better, for example by taking over routine reporting. That idea is explored in leadership in the age of AI.

Where should a leader start?

Pick one process where an agent could take over a clear, repeatable part of the work. Write down the intent and the quality bar. Name an owner. Tell the team that early mistakes are expected and will be discussed, not punished. Review after a month.

That small step teaches more about leading with agents than any strategy deck. It is also the core of the conversation many leadership teams are now having, and a theme in the keynote on leadership and management in the age of AI.

Deepfake CEO fraud: how the fake CFO scam works and how to keep it out

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

In deepfake CEO fraud, criminals use AI-generated video or voice to pose as a senior executive and push an employee into an urgent, secret payment. It exploits authority and time pressure, not technology. According to the FBI, reported business email compromise losses passed US$3 billion in 2025. The best defence is a payment process no voice or face can override.

A few years ago, a fake email from "the CEO" was the classic trick. Now the CEO can appear on a video call, with the right face and the right voice. This article explains how the scam works, how big the problem is, and which simple checks stop it.

What is deepfake CEO fraud?

It is a form of business email compromise, or BEC: fraud aimed at companies that regularly make payments to suppliers. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report describes how criminals compromise email accounts, phone numbers and video meeting apps to get money transferred.

The AI part is new. Chat tools write emails that sound exactly like a company's CEO. Voice cloning copies how an executive speaks. Deepfake video adds a face. The goal stays the same: a transfer that looks approved from the top.

How did the fake CFO video call in Hong Kong work?

The best documented case comes from Hong Kong. In a written reply to the Legislative Council in June 2024, the city's Secretary for Security described it step by step:

  1. At the end of January 2024, an employee received a phishing email from someone posing as the chief financial officer of the company's head office in the United Kingdom.
  2. The employee was invited to a group video conference about "confidential transactions".
  3. Following instructions, the employee authorised transfers to five local bank accounts. The loss was about HK$200 million.
  4. Police believe the video conference was pre-recorded, made from public online video clips and the voice of the executive. There was no real interaction.
  5. The fraudster ended the meeting with an excuse and continued the instructions through an instant messaging app.

The same reply mentions a second case, in May 2024. A staff member of a multinational joined a video call of almost 30 minutes with a "fake boss", who asked for a transfer of nearly HK$4 million.

Notice what made it work: a familiar face, a trusted role, secrecy and speed. No system was hacked.

How big is the problem?

Big, and probably larger than the figures show. In 2025 the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center received 24,768 BEC complaints, with reported losses of over US$3 billion. More than 22,000 complaints in all crime types mentioned AI, with adjusted losses of over US$893 million.

For BEC scams that explicitly involved AI, businesses reported losses of over US$30 million. That sounds modest, but the FBI itself warns that many victims do not realise AI was involved. In investment fraud, for example, losses with a reported AI link were over US$632 million, while total investment fraud losses exceeded US$8 billion.

In Europe, Europol's threat assessment IOCTA 2025 points in the same direction. According to the European Commission's summary, the report highlights a rise in generative AI used to "supercharge" social engineering, with scam messages tailored to the victim's cultural context and personal details.

For a broader look at how AI is used to deceive people, read the new face of deception.

Why do smart, careful people fall for it?

Because the scam is built around normal behaviour. Employees are trained to act quickly when a senior executive asks. A "confidential acquisition" explains why nobody else may know. A video call seems to remove the doubt that a strange email raises. In the Hong Kong case, the fraud started with a phishing email, and the video call made the request look real.

Spotting a deepfake by eye is getting harder. We described why in the death of truth: how deepfakes are rewriting reality. The same voice technology is used against families too, as in AI voice manipulation in virtual kidnapping.

How do you keep the fake CFO out?

Do not rely on people recognising a fake. Rely on a process that makes the fake irrelevant.

  • Verify through a second channel you choose. Call the requester back on a number from your own records, never on a number or link in the message.
  • Require two people for large or unusual payments. No single employee should be able to release a large transfer, however senior the requester seems.
  • Make "secret and urgent" a red flag, not a reason. Write in your policy that confidentiality never overrides the payment checks. Say it out loud from the top.
  • Use a verification word. The FBI advises families to agree on a secret word or phrase to confirm identity. Finance teams can do the same for payment requests.
  • Protect changes to bank details. A new account number for a known supplier always gets a call-back check.
  • Practise. Run an exercise with a fake request, and discuss the result without blame.
  • Report quickly. Speed matters. In 2025 the FBI's recovery team helped freeze over US$679 million in fraudulent transfers, a success rate of 58%, by contacting banks while the money was still moving.

AI is also used on the attacking side in other ways, from phishing to malware. See the growing role of generative AI in cyberattacks.

Does the EU AI Act help against deepfakes?

Partly. The European Commission explains that under the AI Act, deepfakes must be clearly and visibly labelled, and that providers of generative AI must make AI-generated content identifiable. These transparency rules apply from August 2026.

That helps honest users and platforms. Criminals will not label their fakes. The law makes it easier to recognise legitimate AI content, but your payment process still has to stop the illegitimate kind.

What should a board do this quarter?

Ask three questions. Can one person in our company release a large payment after a single call or video meeting? Do our finance staff know they are allowed, and expected, to say no to an executive? And when did we last test it?

If the answer to the first question is yes, that is the place to start. It is also one of the themes in the keynote on cybercrime in the age of AI, because the weak spot is rarely the technology. It is the moment someone decides to trust.

Retail after search: what happens when AI agents do the shopping

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

When AI agents do the shopping, an agent reads your product data, compares you with competitors and pays for the customer. It is growing fast: according to Adobe, traffic from generative AI tools to US retail sites rose 693.4% in the 2025 holiday season. Retailers win when an agent can find, understand and trust their prices, stock and policies.

For twenty years, online retail revolved around search: be found in Google, win the click, convert the visit. That model is starting to shift. Since late 2025, shoppers can ask an AI assistant to find a product and complete the purchase inside the conversation. This article explains what has changed, how big it is today, and what retailers and brands can do before the next holiday season.

What is agentic commerce?

Agentic commerce means that an AI agent does more than give advice. It takes a goal ("a waterproof running jacket under 150 pounds, delivered by Friday"), searches, compares and checks out. If you want a primer on how these agents work in general, see our article on the future of generative AI agents.

Three open standards now make this possible at scale:

  • Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP). Launched by Stripe and OpenAI in September 2025 together with Instant Checkout in ChatGPT, which let US users buy from Etsy sellers without leaving the chat. Stripe said over a million Shopify merchants would follow (Stripe).
  • Agent Payments Protocol (AP2). Announced by Google in September 2025 with more than 60 organisations, including Mastercard, PayPal, American Express and Adyen (Google Cloud).
  • Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP). Launched by Google in January 2026 and co-developed with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair, Target and Walmart, to cover the whole journey from discovery and buying to post-purchase support (Google).

The fact that competitors are agreeing on open standards is the clearest signal that this is not a passing experiment.

How big is it already?

Still small, but growing fast. According to Adobe, traffic from generative AI tools to US retail sites rose by 693.4% during the 2025 holiday season compared with a year earlier, based on more than a trillion visits to US retail sites. Adobe adds a caveat: "the base of users remains modest".

The supply side is moving faster. In September 2026, Google reported that UCP "already enables direct checkout for hundreds of thousands of brands and retailers across Google" (Google). In other words, the checkout is ready before most shoppers are. That makes the 2026 holiday season the first in which hundreds of thousands of brands can be bought from directly inside an AI assistant.

What changes for retailers and brands?

Your product data becomes your shop window

A human can live with a vague product page. An agent cannot. It compares structured facts: price, size, material, delivery time, return policy, stock. If those are missing or inconsistent, the agent simply picks a competitor whose data is complete. Google says that merchants adopting its core Merchant Center feed best practices see on average a 5% increase in conversions the following month (Google).

The brand has to persuade the person and the agent

Loyalty and brand preference do not disappear, but they need to be explicit. If a customer tells an assistant "I always buy from this brand", the agent follows that. If not, the agent optimises for whatever the customer asked. We explored the consumer side of this question in are you buying what you want, or is AI in control?

The retailer stays the seller

The new protocols are designed so that retailers keep the customer relationship. The ACP specification states that "businesses maintain their customer relationships as the merchant of record, retaining control over which products can be sold, how they're presented, and how orders are fulfilled" (Agentic Commerce Protocol). Whether that holds in practice, when the conversation itself happens on someone else's platform, is one of the big open questions.

Who pays, and who is accountable when an agent gets it wrong?

Payments are where trust is won or lost. Stripe's Shared Payment Token "lets applications like ChatGPT initiate a payment without exposing the buyer's payment credentials" (Stripe). Google's AP2 adds signed "mandates": an Intent Mandate records what the user asked for, and a Cart Mandate records the exact items and price the user approved. Google frames the problem as three questions: authorisation (did the user give the agent this authority?), authenticity (does the request reflect the user's real intent?) and accountability (who is responsible if a transaction is fraudulent or wrong?) (Google Cloud).

For retailers, this means returns, disputes and fraud checks need a new path for orders placed by agents. For more on where payments are heading, see the future of finance and AI managing our money.

What happens to the physical store?

It stays, but its job changes. In Great Britain, 28.8% of retail sales were made online in September 2026, according to the Office for National Statistics. More than seven in ten pounds are still spent elsewhere. Agents will take over the routine, repeat purchases first: groceries, household goods, replacements. That leaves the store with what an agent cannot do well: let people touch, try, ask and be inspired.

At the same time, retailers are becoming media companies. In the US, commerce media revenue reached $63.4 billion in 2025, up 18% on the year before (IAB and PwC). If agents skip the ads on a product page, that model will have to change too. We looked at the longer arc of these shifts in the future of retail: 10 examples, which now includes a section on retail in 2026. For organisations that want to explore what this means for their own strategy, see Richard's keynotes for the retail sector.

What can retailers do now?

  1. Audit your product data. Check that price, stock, size, material, delivery time and return policy are complete and consistent in every feed.
  2. Decide where you want to be bought. Find out which AI assistants and protocols your platform and payment provider already support.
  3. Prepare for agent orders. Agree how you handle returns, disputes and fraud checks when an agent placed the order.
  4. Measure AI traffic separately. Visits and sales from AI assistants behave differently from search traffic; track them as their own channel.
  5. Give the store a reason to exist. Invest in advice, experience and service that an agent cannot replace.

The shift from search to agents will not happen overnight. But the standards, the payment rails and the first checkouts are already live. Retailers that make themselves readable for agents this year will be the ones those agents recommend next year.

  • Trendwatcher. Kijkt vooral naar wat nu verandert: nieuw gedrag van klanten, nieuwe technologie, nieuwe bedrijfsmodellen. Trendwatching richt zich vaak op de komende jaren en op de vraag wat dat betekent voor een merk of markt.
  • Futurist of futuroloog. Kijkt verder vooruit, vaak tien jaar of meer, en verbindt trends uit verschillende hoeken: technologie, economie, klimaat, bevolking. Futuroloog is het oudere Nederlandse woord, futurist het woord dat je internationaal hoort.
  • Toekomstonderzoeker. Werkt meestal aan een universiteit, een planbureau of een overheid. Dit vak heet internationaal futures studies of strategic foresight, en het werk is vaak systematischer en beter gedocumenteerd. De Universiteit van Turku in Finland heeft er bijvoorbeeld een eigen onderzoekscentrum voor, het Finland Futures Research Centre.

Het verschil zit dus minder in het etiket dan in de vraag: hoe ver kijk je vooruit, en hoe onderbouw je wat je zegt?

Voorspelt een futurist de toekomst?

Nee, en een goede futurist zal dat ook niet beweren. De Europese Commissie zegt het zo: foresight gaat niet over het voorspellen van de toekomst, maar is "de discipline van het verkennen van verschillende mogelijke toekomsten", met de kansen en problemen die daarbij horen. Het doel is een betere toekomst helpen vormen.

De Commissie gebruikt daarvoor het beeld van een trechter met vier soorten toekomsten: mogelijk, plausibel, waarschijnlijk en wenselijk. Een futurist laat zien hoe breed die trechter is, en helpt je kiezen welke toekomst je wilt nastreven.

Hoe werkt een futurist of trendwatcher?

Het werk bestaat grofweg uit vier stappen. De Europese Commissie beschrijft dezelfde methoden voor haar eigen beleid.

1. Signalen verzamelen

Alles begint met kijken. Wat gebeurt er in start-ups, laboratoria, wetgeving en klantgedrag dat nog klein is, maar groot kan worden? De Commissie noemt dit horizon scanning: vroeg de mogelijke "game changers" opsporen en de vroege signalen wegen. Hoe je zulke kleine verschuivingen herkent, lees je in Microtrends herkennen kan het verschil betekenen tussen succes en mislukking.

2. Trends duiden

Een los signaal zegt weinig. Pas als signalen een richting laten zien, spreek je van een trend. Daarboven staan megatrends: krachten op lange termijn die nu al zichtbaar zijn en waarschijnlijk wereldwijd effect hebben. Zo omschrijft het Competence Centre on Foresight van het Joint Research Centre ze in zijn Megatrends Hub. Het centrum volgt er 14, waaronder snellere technologische verandering, het veranderende karakter van werk en klimaatverandering. Welke megatrends wij voor het bedrijfsleven zien, staat in Voorbij AI: vijf megatrends die het bedrijfsleven de komende tien jaar gaan opschudden.

3. Scenario's maken

Omdat de toekomst onzeker is, werk je met meerdere verhalen naast elkaar. Een Nederlands voorbeeld is de Toekomstverkenning WLO van het Planbureau voor de Leefomgeving. De onderzoekers maakten vier scenario's voor Nederland in 2040, 2050 en 2060. Ze kozen twee assen: hoge of lage economische groei, en een snelle of vertraagde klimaattransitie. Geen van de vier is een voorspelling; samen laten ze zien waar beleid tegen bestand moet zijn.

4. Toetsen

De laatste stap is de belangrijkste: wat betekent dit voor jouw plannen? De Commissie gebruikt scenario's om beleid te "stresstesten": werkt een plan ook als de wereld anders loopt dan verwacht? Een bedrijf kan zijn strategie, productportfolio of investeringen op dezelfde manier langs een paar scenario's leggen.

What does a futurist or trend watcher do, and when should you bring one in?

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

A futurist or trend watcher helps an organisation think ahead by collecting signals, interpreting trends and building scenarios. Prediction is not the goal. According to the European Commission, foresight means exploring different possible futures, and its Joint Research Centre tracks 14 global megatrends for this. You bring one in for strategy, innovation or an audience.

This article explains the profession without a list of names. You will read what the different titles mean, what the work looks like and which questions to ask before you hire someone.

What is the difference between a futurist, a trend watcher and a foresight researcher?

In practice the titles overlap, and the lines between them are not sharp. Still, each puts the emphasis somewhere else.

  • Trend watcher. Looks mainly at what is changing now: new customer behaviour, new technology, new business models. Trendwatching often focuses on the next few years and on what that means for a brand or market.
  • Futurist. Looks further ahead, often ten years or more, and connects trends from different areas: technology, the economy, climate, population. In some languages you will also hear the older word futurologist.
  • Foresight researcher. Usually works at a university, a government or a public research body. The field is known as futures studies or strategic foresight, and the work tends to be more systematic and better documented. The University of Turku, for example, has its own research centre for it, the Finland Futures Research Centre.

So the difference lies less in the label than in two questions: how far ahead do you look, and how do you back up what you say?

Does a futurist predict the future?

No, and a good futurist will not claim to. The European Commission puts it this way: foresight "is not about predicting or forecasting the future. It is the discipline of exploring different possible futures", including the challenges and opportunities they may bring. The aim is to help shape a preferable future.

The Commission uses the image of a futures cone with four classes of futures: possible, plausible, probable and preferable. A futurist shows how wide that cone is, and helps you decide which future you want to work towards.

How does a futurist or trend watcher work?

Roughly, the work has four steps. The European Commission describes the same methods for its own policy work.

1. Collecting signals

It all starts with looking. What is happening in start-ups, labs, legislation and customer behaviour that is still small but could become big? The Commission calls this horizon scanning: detecting emerging "game changers" at an early stage and weighing the early signals. How to recognise such small shifts is the subject of Microtrends can decide who wins or loses in business.

2. Interpreting trends

A single signal says little. Only when signals point in one direction do you speak of a trend. Above trends sit megatrends: long-term driving forces that are observable now and will most likely have a global impact. That is how the Joint Research Centre's Competence Centre on Foresight defines them in its Megatrends Hub. It tracks 14 of them, including accelerating technological change, the changing nature of work and climate change. The megatrends we see for business are in Beyond AI: five mega trends that will reshape business in the next decade.

3. Building scenarios

Because the future is uncertain, you work with several stories side by side. A Dutch example is the WLO 2025 outlook by PBL Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency. Its researchers built four scenarios for the Netherlands in 2040, 2050 and 2060, along two axes: high or lower economic growth, and a fast or delayed climate transition. None of the four is a forecast; together they show what policy needs to withstand.

4. Stress-testing

The last step matters most: what does this mean for your plans? The Commission uses scenarios to "stress-test" policy options: does a plan still work if the world turns out differently than expected? A company can run its strategy, product portfolio or investments past a few scenarios in the same way.

What does an organisation gain from a futurist or trend watcher?

It depends on your question. Broadly, there are three reasons to bring someone in.

  • Strategy. You want to know which developments will affect your market over the next ten years, and whether your plans can withstand them. A trend analysis and a scenario session with the leadership team fit here.
  • Innovation. You are looking for new products or services. A trend watcher helps you see opportunities that are still small, and test ideas against where customers are heading.
  • Inspiration for an audience. At a conference or kick-off you want people to look at things differently. A keynote about the future shows what is coming their way and starts a conversation.

Governments take this seriously. The European Commission has published a Strategic Foresight Report every year since the first one in 2020, and each EU member state designates a "Minister for the Future" who meets the others at least once a year. UNESCO speaks of futures literacy: helping people and institutions use the future to navigate uncertainty and make better decisions in the present. That is a capability an organisation can build, even without a futurist on the payroll.

When should you bring in a futurist or trend watcher?

A few moments stand out:

  • you are starting a new strategy or a multi-year plan;
  • your market is changing fast, for example because of AI, new regulation or new competitors;
  • your team is stuck in the current year and needs a fresh view from outside;
  • you are organising a conference, anniversary or kick-off where people need to be challenged.

A futurist does not replace your own research. See them as someone who widens the view and asks the right questions; the follow-up work is yours.

How do you choose a futurist or trend watcher?

Do not choose on name or follower count, but on what you want to do differently afterwards. Ask these questions up front:

  1. What is the goal? Inspiring a large audience calls for something different than a working session with ten board members.
  2. How far ahead should it look? Two years (trendwatching) or ten to twenty years (futures work and scenarios)?
  3. How does the speaker back up the story? Ask about sources, examples and how current the material is.
  4. Do they know your sector? General trends are interesting, but examples from your own market are what people remember.
  5. What is left afterwards? A presentation, a trend report, a follow-up session?

Frequently asked questions

Is a futurologist the same as a futurist?

In practice, yes. Both look further ahead than a trend watcher usually does.

Can a futurist predict the future?

No. According to the European Commission, foresight is precisely not about predicting, but about exploring several possible futures. A futurist helps you prepare for different outcomes.

What is strategic foresight?

Strategic foresight is the structured version of the same work, used by governments and companies: horizon scanning, megatrend analysis and scenarios, applied to real decisions. The European Commission uses it to stress-test its policy against different futures.

How do you recognise a good trend watcher?

Look at how they back things up: which sources they use, how current their examples are, and whether they discuss several possible outcomes instead of one confident prediction.

Can you do trendwatching yourself?

Yes. Start by following a fixed set of sources, collecting signals and discussing them with your team every quarter. UNESCO calls this capability futures literacy: learning to use the future to make better decisions.

From chatbot to AI agent: what customer service will look like by 2029

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

AI agents are moving customer service from answering questions to resolving cases, such as refunds, returns and cancellations. The effect can be large: according to Klarna's own filing with the US SEC, its AI assistant handled 69% of customer service chats in the twelve months to June 2025. In the EU, customers must be told they are talking to AI.

This article explains what changes when a chatbot becomes an agent, what the numbers show so far, what European rules already require, and how to make the move without losing your customers' trust. One honest note up front: nobody can measure 2029 yet. Where we describe the future, we say whose forecast it is, or that it is an expectation.

What is the difference between a chatbot and an AI agent?

A classic chatbot answers questions. It looks up an answer in a script or a knowledge base and hands the customer over when it gets stuck. An AI agent goes a step further: it understands the request, plans the steps and carries them out in your systems. It can check an order, start a refund, change a delivery address or cancel a subscription, and then confirm what it did.

That shift matters for customer service, because most contacts are not questions but errands. A customer does not want to know how a refund works. They want the refund.

Agents are getting better at this kind of work. According to Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index Report, AI agents went from 12% to around 66% task success on OSWorld, a test of real computer tasks. They still fail roughly 1 in 3 attempts on structured benchmarks. For customer service, that means agents are useful for clear, repeatable cases, and still need a safety net.

We first wrote about this step in beyond chatbots: the exciting future of generative AI agents.

What do the numbers show so far?

The best documented example comes from the payments company Klarna. Its numbers are its own, so read them as company figures, not independent research.

In a press release from February 2024, one month after launch, Klarna reported that its AI assistant:

  • had 2.3 million conversations, two-thirds of its customer service chats;
  • did the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents;
  • was on par with human agents on customer satisfaction;
  • led to a 25% drop in repeat inquiries;
  • helped customers resolve errands in less than 2 minutes, compared to 11 minutes before;
  • worked in 23 markets, 24/7, in more than 35 languages.

A year and a half later, in its registration statement for the US stock market (Form F-1, September 2025), Klarna wrote that the assistant handled 69% of customer service chats in the twelve months ended 30 June 2025, according to its chat log data. It said the assistant did the work of over 700 full-time agents and delivered approximately $39 million in cost savings in 2024. Queries were resolved in two minutes, compared to the 12-minute average for human agents in 2024. Since launch, the assistant had 25.3 million conversations.

Two details in the same filing are worth noting. First, Klarna continues to offer all customers the option to talk to a human, because "certain consumers may nevertheless prefer to interact with human representatives". It calls this a "dual-track approach" of scalable AI combined with high-quality human support. Second, the numbers are about chats. They say nothing about the hardest cases, which tend to end up with people.

Klarna is not alone in moving fast. Stanford HAI reports that organisational adoption of AI reached 88%. That does not mean 88% of companies use agents in customer service, but it shows how quickly AI is becoming part of normal operations.

Who is responsible when an AI agent gets it wrong?

The company is. A Canadian case shows this clearly. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149), decided on 14 February 2024, a customer had relied on the airline's website chatbot. The chatbot suggested he could apply for a bereavement fare after his trip. Air Canada's actual policy did not allow that.

Air Canada argued it could not be held liable for information from its chatbot. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal called that "a remarkable submission" and wrote: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." The tribunal found that Air Canada did not take reasonable care to make sure its chatbot was accurate, and ordered it to pay a total of $812.02, including $650.88 in damages.

The amount was small. The lesson is not. An agent that can act, not just talk, raises the stakes further: a wrong answer becomes a wrong refund, a wrong cancellation or a wrong promise.

What do EU rules require from AI in customer service?

Several rules already apply, or will apply soon.

Customers must know they are talking to AI. Under Article 50 of the AI Act, AI systems that interact directly with people must be designed so that people are informed they are interacting with an AI system, unless that is obvious from the context. That information must be clear and given at the latest at the time of the first interaction. According to the European Commission, the transparency rules come into effect in August 2026. The Commission gives chatbots as the example: humans should be made aware that they are interacting with a machine.

In financial services, customers can ask for a human. A new EU directive on financial services sold at a distance, Directive (EU) 2023/2673, applies from 19 June 2026, according to the European Commission. Each country writes it into its own law. Ireland's version, for example, S.I. No. 309/2026, gives a consumer using a trader's online interface "a right to request and to obtain human intervention" before signing, and in justified cases afterwards.

Some decisions are high-risk. Most customer service bots fall under the transparency rules. But the AI Act treats certain uses as high-risk, such as AI that decides access to essential private and public services, for example credit scoring. According to the Commission, strict obligations for these systems apply from 2 December 2027, including human oversight and logging of activity.

Staff need to understand the tools. Article 4 of the AI Act asks organisations that use AI to support the AI literacy of their staff. The European Commission explains that this has applied since 2 February 2025, and that national authorities supervise it from 2 August 2026. For customer service teams that work alongside agents, this is practical, not just legal: people who understand an agent's limits catch its mistakes.

What will customer service look like by 2029?

Here it helps to separate what is fixed from what is a forecast.

What is fixed. By the end of 2027, the rules above all apply in the EU: disclosure of AI, a right to human intervention in financial services sold online, and strict obligations for high-risk uses. Any agent a company builds now has to work within them in 2029.

What the trend suggests. Agents are improving quickly, and companies like Klarna already handle a majority of chats with AI. If both trends continue, it is reasonable to expect that by 2029 routine errands will more often be resolved end to end by an agent, while people focus on exceptions, complaints, vulnerable customers and the cases where judgement and empathy matter. That is an expectation, not a measurement.

How do you move from chatbot to agent without losing customers?

A few principles follow directly from the examples and rules above:

  • Start with one type of errand. Pick a high-volume, low-risk case with clear rules, such as order status or address changes. Not complaints or credit decisions.
  • Say it is AI, at the start. Not hidden in the terms, but in the first message. That is required in the EU, and it builds trust.
  • Always offer a route to a person. Klarna does it by choice. In financial services sold online it is a legal right. Make the route easy to find.
  • Limit what the agent may do. Decide which actions it can take on its own, which need a confirmation, and which stay with people. The cost of a mistake decides, not the demo.
  • Check what it says about your policies. The Air Canada case was about a wrong policy answer. Test the agent on your own rules, especially exceptions.
  • Measure resolution, not deflection. A chat that ends is not a solved problem. Repeat contacts, as Klarna reported, are a better signal.
  • Train the team. People who work with agents need to know what they do well, where they fail and how to step in.

Will AI agents replace customer service staff?

They are already taking over a large share of routine chats. Klarna says its assistant does the work of over 700 full-time agents. At the same time, Klarna still offers every customer the option to talk to a human. The likely change is in the work itself: fewer routine contacts, more complex and sensitive cases, and a new task of supervising agents.

In the EU, yes, unless it is obvious from the context. Article 50 of the AI Act requires that people are informed they are interacting with an AI system, at the latest at the first interaction. The transparency rules apply from August 2026.

Can a company be held liable for what its chatbot says?

Yes. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, the tribunal ruled that a company is responsible for all the information on its website, whether it comes from a static page or a chatbot. The airline had to pay the customer back.

Usually not. Most customer service bots fall under the transparency rules. Some uses are high-risk, such as AI that decides access to essential services like credit. Those strict obligations apply from 2 December 2027.

Where should you start?

Look at your top ten reasons for contact and pick the one that is most rule-based and least risky. Write down what the agent may do on its own, when it hands over to a person and how customers reach that person. Tell customers it is AI. Review the results after a month, with your service team at the table.

An AI policy for your school: the decisions school leaders cannot skip

Richard van Hooijdonk · datum volgt · concept

A school AI policy needs five decisions: which tools are allowed, how assessment changes, how pupil data is protected, what staff need to learn, and who keeps the policy up to date. Many schools have not made them: according to Stanford HAI, only half of US secondary schools have AI policies, and only 6% of teachers find them clear.

Pupils are not waiting for the policy. This article walks through the five decisions, what European rules already require of schools, and what a short, workable policy can look like. It is written for school leaders, not for IT departments.

Why does a school need an AI policy now?

Because the use is already there, and the guidance is not. According to Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index Report, over 80% of US high school and college students now use AI for school-related tasks. Yet only half of middle and high schools have AI policies in place, and just 6% of teachers say those policies are clear. Stanford sums it up as: formal education is lagging behind AI.

The gap is not new. In 2023, a UNESCO global survey of over 450 schools and universities found that less than 10% had institutional policies or formal guidance on generative AI, largely because national rules were missing.

In Europe, the rules are no longer missing. Parts of the EU AI Act already apply to schools, and more will follow (see below). A policy is how a school turns those rules, and its own values, into everyday choices for teachers and pupils.

Decision 1: Which AI tools are allowed, and for whom?

Start with a short list of approved tools, and say what each may be used for. Teachers need to know what they can use for lesson preparation. Pupils need to know what they can use for homework, and when they cannot.

Age matters. UNESCO's Guidance for generative AI in education and research (2023) sets an age limit of 13 for the use of AI tools in the classroom, and proposes an age-appropriate approach. UNESCO also warns that many educational institutions are "largely unprepared to validate the tools". So build in a simple check before a new tool is used with pupils: who made it, what data does it collect, and what happens to that data?

Some uses are simply off limits. Under Article 5 of the AI Act, AI systems that infer the emotions of people in education institutions are prohibited, except for medical or safety reasons. According to the European Commission, this ban has applied since February 2025. A tool that claims to measure how engaged or stressed pupils are by reading their faces does not belong in a European classroom.

Decision 2: How does assessment change when every pupil has AI?

This is the decision most schools put off, and the one teachers ask about first. If a take-home essay can be written by AI in seconds, it no longer shows what a pupil can do on their own. Schools have three broad options, and most combine them: assess more of the process (drafts, oral explanations, work in class), allow AI explicitly and ask pupils to show how they used it, or keep certain tasks AI-free and supervised.

Whatever you choose, write it down per type of task, so pupils do not get different rules from every teacher. We looked at the student side of this in almost every college student is cheating with AI, so now what?

There is also a legal line. The AI Act classifies certain AI systems in education as high-risk. According to Annex III, these include AI used to decide admission, to evaluate learning outcomes, to assess the level of education a pupil will receive, and to monitor pupils for prohibited behaviour during tests. The European Commission says strict obligations for these high-risk uses apply from 2 December 2027, including human oversight. A school that lets AI grade exams, or uses AI proctoring, should know this before signing a contract.

Decision 3: How do you protect pupil data?

Many AI tools learn from what users type in. In a school, that can mean names, grades, special needs or family situations. UNESCO's guidance warns that without proper rules, the data privacy of users is left unprotected, and calls for data protection and privacy standards.

A policy can make three things concrete:

  • what staff and pupils may never enter into an AI tool (for example personal data about pupils);
  • which tools have been checked and agreed with the school's data protection officer;
  • who to contact when something goes wrong.

Decision 4: What do teachers and staff need to learn?

A policy only works if people understand the tools. In the EU, this is also a legal point. Article 4 of the AI Act asks organisations that deploy AI systems to take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff. According to the European Commission, this has applied since 2 February 2025, and national market surveillance authorities supervise it from 2 August 2026.

The rule was softened this year. Since the Digital Omnibus entered into force in mid-July 2026, AI literacy remains an obligation, but no specific or "sufficient" level is required. There is no need for a certificate either: organisations can keep an internal record of trainings. For schools that use high-risk AI systems, the obligation to train staff for human oversight remains in place.

For the content, schools do not have to start from scratch. On 18 June 2026, the European Commission and the OECD presented an AI Literacy Framework for primary and secondary education. It is meant for teachers, education leaders and policymakers and is organised around four domains: engage with AI, create with AI, manage AI and shape AI. The Commission's Q&A notes that the framework is non-binding.

UNESCO also calls for teacher training. In practice, a short session per team on the approved tools, what they do well and where they fail is a better start than a long course nobody finishes.

Decision 5: Who owns the policy, and how often is it reviewed?

AI tools change every few months. A policy written once and filed away is out of date within a year. Name an owner, for example a member of the leadership team, and agree on a review moment, at least once a school year. Involve teachers, pupils and parents: they see in practice where the policy works and where it does not.

What should a one-page AI policy contain?

A workable policy can fit on one page:

  1. Purpose. Why the school uses AI, and what it wants to protect (learning, fairness, privacy).
  2. Approved tools. A short list, per group (staff, pupils by age), with what each is for.
  3. Red lines. No personal data in unapproved tools; no emotion recognition; no AI as the only judge of a grade.
  4. Assessment. Per type of task: AI allowed, allowed with explanation, or not allowed.
  5. Training. What every staff member learns, and how it is recorded.
  6. Owner and review. Who is responsible, and when the policy is next reviewed.

Frequently asked questions

Should schools ban AI?

A ban is hard to enforce when over 80% of US high school and college students already use AI for schoolwork, according to Stanford HAI. UNESCO argues for a human-centred approach with clear rules, age limits and teacher training, rather than ignoring the tools. Some uses, like emotion recognition, are banned in the EU anyway.

Is AI in schools high-risk under the EU AI Act?

Not all of it. Using AI to prepare lessons is not on the high-risk list. AI that decides admission, evaluates learning outcomes, sets the level of education or monitors pupils during tests is high-risk under Annex III. Strict obligations for those uses apply from 2 December 2027.

Do teachers need an AI certificate?

No. The European Commission states there is no need for a certificate. Schools that use AI must support the AI literacy of their staff, and can keep an internal record of trainings.

From what age can pupils use AI tools?

UNESCO's guidance sets an age limit of 13 for the use of AI tools in the classroom. Check the age rules of each tool as well, and set your own limits per age group in the policy.

Where should a school leader start?

Put the five decisions on the agenda of one leadership meeting. Make a first choice for each, write it on one page, and share it with the team as a draft. Ask teachers and pupils for feedback within a month. A clear, imperfect policy helps more than a perfect one that never arrives.
