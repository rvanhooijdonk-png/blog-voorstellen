How to choose a guest speaker for your event, and get the most out of the talk

To choose the right guest speaker, start at least six months ahead, know who will be in the room, and write down in one sentence what that audience should think or do differently afterwards. Set a budget, watch each candidate give a full talk and check their references. Then make the session work: brief the speaker, build in interaction and measure what people took away.

Key takeaways

  • Start at least six months before the event, so you have time to compare candidates.
  • Pick a speaker for your audience, not for their fame. A name that works for one room can fall flat in another.
  • Define one goal and a clear budget, and ask what a quote includes: preparation, tailoring, travel, recording rights.
  • Watch a recent full-length talk, interview the speaker and call organisers of comparable events.
  • Audiences want to take part. In a YouGov survey of 601 meeting professionals for American Express Global Business Travel, 42% said attendees want more interactive sessions than five years ago.
  • Measure afterwards, including the "return on experience": did people leave engaged, inspired and with something new?

What a guest speaker adds to your event

A good guest speaker gives your audience a fresh perspective, starts the conversations you want people to have, and makes the event easier to remember. They connect theory and practice: a speaker who works with a subject every day can explain it with real examples, which makes it easier to understand and easier to act on.

The right choice also makes your event look more important, helps registrations and keeps the room engaged until the end. The wrong choice costs more than the fee. It can alienate your audience and make them think twice about coming back.

Start early: six months is not too soon

Organising an event means choosing a venue, a theme and a programme, setting a budget and getting to know your audience, and only then inviting speakers. Leave yourself enough time to research and compare options before you decide. Good speakers are booked months ahead, so start your search at least six months before the event. Our guide on how to hire the perfect speaker walks you through the process step by step.

Choose for your audience

A common mistake is to book a popular name in the hope of attracting attention, without looking at who will be in the room. A speaker who is a hit with young entrepreneurs may not land with a board of directors, and the other way round. Find out as much as you can about the people attending: their roles, their sector, what they already know about the topic and what they expect from the day. Once you know that, you also know what to look for in a keynote speaker.

Expectations are also changing. According to the 2026 Global Meetings & Events Forecast of American Express Global Business Travel, five generations now work side by side, and organisers are moving away from long, marathon-style sessions.

Set one goal and a clear budget

Decide what the event should achieve. Do you want to inform people about market trends, support a change programme, spark innovation or simply inspire? The goal decides the type of speaker. Sometimes a well-informed industry insider is a better choice than a celebrity, and sometimes the reverse is true. Write the goal down in one sentence and ask every candidate how they would tailor their talk to it.

Then set the budget and adjust your expectations to it. When we first wrote about this in 2019, guest speakers in the United States cost roughly between $2,000 and $30,000, and celebrity speakers started at $50,000. Fees have moved since, so treat these figures as an indication only. Ask for a written quote and check what it includes: preparation, tailoring to your audience, travel and accommodation, audio-visual requirements, and the right to record or share the talk. A higher fee does not automatically mean a better talk; you may also find a strong speaker who is not yet a household name.

Where to find the right candidates

  • Your own experience: think back to events you attended and the speakers who held your attention.
  • Your network: ask colleagues and other organisers who they have seen or worked with.
  • Comparable events: look at the programmes of conferences similar to yours.
  • Professional networks and bureaus: the Global Speakers Federation brings together 17 independent speaker associations from more than 20 countries, and the International Association of Speakers Bureaus connects agencies worldwide. Working through a bureau can cost more, but it saves you search time.

Check before you book

  1. Watch them speak. See a recent full-length talk, in person or on video, and not only a showreel. It tells you more about tone and depth than any profile.
  2. Talk to them. A call or video meeting shows how well they listen to your goal and how they would adapt their content.
  3. Check references. Ask for contacts at comparable events and call them. Check whether the claims in the speaker's bio can be verified.
  4. Look at their reach. A speaker with an active audience on social media can help you promote the event.

Brief the speaker

Tell the speaker in advance what you expect: the goal, the audience, the length of the talk, and whether there is a Q&A, a panel or a workshop afterwards. Agree on the format, on stage, online or hybrid, and on the language. Can the speaker present fluently in the language of your audience and handle questions in it? Make the session interactive

Audiences want to take part. In the Amex GBT forecast, based on a YouGov survey of 601 meeting professionals in eight countries (July 2025), 42% said attendees now want more interactive sessions, such as workshops, than five years ago, and 40% said they want more social activities and networking. Sessions are getting shorter, to make room for longer networking breaks.

  • Live polls and quizzes: event technology lets the audience respond during the talk, and gives the speaker real-time feedback.
  • Q&A: plan time for questions at the end, so people can relate the content to their own situation.
  • Workshops and breakouts: hands-on sessions after the keynote help people apply what they heard.
  • Networking: plan meet-and-greet time with the speaker and space for attendees to talk to each other.
  • Storytelling: a talk built on strong stories and clear visuals stays with people longer.

Before and after the event

A guest speaker can also bring attention to your event outside the room. Prepare a media kit with high-resolution photos, the speaker's bio and the main themes of the talk, and link those themes to current news when you approach journalists. Create an event hashtag and invite the speaker to share it. After the event, send attendees the key takeaways, so the conversation continues.

Measure what it delivered

Go back to the goal you wrote down. Measure the outcome with a short feedback survey, internal indicators or employee engagement scores. The Amex GBT forecast also suggests looking at the "return on experience": did attendees walk away feeling engaged and inspired, and did they learn something new? Those answers tell you more about the value of the speaker than the number of chairs that were filled.

Checklist: compare two or three speakers on these points

  • Audience: who is in the room, how many people, and what do they already know?
  • Goal: what should people think or do differently afterwards, in one sentence?
  • Language: can the speaker present and take questions in your audience's language?
  • Length and format: keynote only, with Q&A, or with a workshop? On stage, online or hybrid?
  • Budget: a written quote, with preparation, tailoring, travel and recording rights spelled out.
  • References: a recent full-length talk, contacts at comparable events, and verifiable claims.

In closing

The success of an event can rest largely on the choice of guest speaker. Choose for your audience and your goal rather than for the biggest name on a list, check what you are buying, and design the session so people can take part. Then a guest speaker does what you booked them for: people leave with a new perspective and something they can act on.
