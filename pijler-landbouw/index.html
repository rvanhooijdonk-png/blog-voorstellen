What is autonomous farming?

Autonomous farming is the use of machines that carry out field work with little or no direct human control. Tractors, sprayers, mowers and robots use satellite positioning, cameras, lidar and AI to find their way, detect obstacles and do the job, while a farmer supervises one or several machines from a phone or tablet. It is the next step after the auto-steer systems many farmers already use: those steer the tractor, but a person still sits in the cab.

That first step is already widespread. According to the USDA Economic Research Service, auto-steer and guidance systems were used on 58.4% of US corn acres in 2016 and on 64.5% of cotton acres in 2019, with the largest farms adopting them fastest. Autonomous agriculture builds on that base, but mechanisation itself is spread very unevenly: the FAO notes that Japan had more than 400 tractors per 1,000 hectares of arable land, compared with just 0.4 in Ghana.

Autonomous farm equipment and agricultural robots in 2026

The large machinery makers now all sell or test autonomous farm equipment. At CES 2025, John Deere showed an autonomous 9RX tractor for large-scale tillage, with a second-generation kit of 16 cameras for a 360-degree view of the field, and an autonomous 5ML orchard tractor that adds lidar sensors to cope with dense canopies. In August 2025, Deere acquired GUSS Automation, whose autonomous sprayers for orchards and vineyards let a single operator manage up to eight machines. More than 250 GUSS machines have been deployed, together spraying 2.6 million acres.

Retrofit kits are moving into the mainstream as well. AGCO's OutRun kit turns existing row-crop tractors into driverless grain carts at harvest time; it works on John Deere 8R and 8000R tractors and is due on Fendt models in 2026. CNH previewed its New Holland R4 robot series at Agritechnica in November 2025: compact electric and hybrid robots that mow, till and spray between the rows of vineyards and orchards.

Smaller agricultural robots for weeding, picking and milking form a second, fast-growing category, as our overview of the top 10 agricultural robots shows. According to the International Federation of Robotics, close to 19,500 agricultural robots were sold worldwide in 2024, and its World Robotics 2026 figures show sales in agriculture growing by 19% in 2025. Those are modest numbers for a global industry. In 2026, autonomous farming is still an early market, found mainly in high-value crops and on large farms, where labour is scarce and the investment is easiest to earn back.

Rules and regulation for autonomous tractors

Regulation is catching up with the technology. In the European Union, the new Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies on a mandatory basis from 20 January 2027. According to the European Commission, it integrates provisions for machinery with AI-powered safety functions and for the cyber-safety of software and safety control systems. That matters for autonomous tractors and field robots, which depend on software to see, decide and stop.

Beyond the rulebook, the barriers are practical. The FAO warns that limited rural infrastructure, such as connectivity and electricity, hinders the spread of digital automation, especially in low- and middle-income countries, and that uneven adoption can deepen inequalities if small-scale producers are left out. For farmers and agribusinesses, the question is therefore less whether autonomous farming will arrive and more which tasks to automate first, and on what terms.
